## Does a positive result mean “diseased”?

Imagine a fictional test: it detects 99% of diseased people and is negative for 95% of people without the disease. If disease prevalence is 1%, what fraction of positive people actually have the disease? Answering 99% confuses two conditional probabilities. We will count first, then derive the result.

Prerequisite: [fractions and proportions](/achraf-notebooks/en/foundations/numbers/). Goals: represent events, distinguish independence from disjointness, calculate conditional probability and derive Bayes’ theorem. These fictional numbers do not evaluate a real medical test.

## Describing a random experiment

The sample space $\Omega$ contains possible outcomes. An **event** $A$ is a set of outcomes. Its complement $A^c$ means “not $A$”. The intersection $A\cap B$ means “$A$ and $B$”; the union $A\cup B$ means “$A$ or $B$, including both”.

A probability $P$ assigns numbers between 0 and 1 to events. It satisfies $P(\Omega)=1$ and additivity for disjoint events. For a countable family of pairwise disjoint events, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities. In particular, $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$ and

$$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).$$

Subtract the intersection because it was counted twice. For a fair die, $A=\{2,4,6\}$ and $B=\{4,5,6\}$ give $P(A\cup B)=3/6+3/6-2/6=4/6$. No independence assumption is needed.

## Conditioning changes the reference group

If $B$ is known to occur and $P(B)>0$, compare outcomes with the group $B$ rather than all of $\Omega$. The fraction of that group also in $A$ is

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.$$

The vertical bar means “given”. For the die above, $P(A\mid B)=(2/6)/(3/6)=2/3$. Two of the three numbers $\{4,5,6\}$ are even.

Events are **independent** when $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. If $P(B)>0$, this is equivalent to $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. **Disjoint** events have an empty intersection. If both have positive probabilities, they cannot be independent: learning that one occurred makes the other impossible.

## Deriving Bayes rather than memorizing it

The definition gives $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$ and also $P(A\cap B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)$ whenever these conditionals exist. Equating and dividing by $P(B)$ gives

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}.$$

Separate $A$ and $A^c$ to calculate the denominator:

$$P(B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A^c)P(A^c).$$

This law of total probability counts every way to observe $B$. For several disjoint groups covering the population, sum their contributions.

## Counting 10,000 people

Let $D$ denote disease and $+$ a positive result. **Prevalence** is $P(D)=0.01$, **sensitivity** is $P(+\mid D)=0.99$, and **specificity** is $P(-\mid D^c)=0.95$. Thus the false-positive rate is $P(+\mid D^c)=0.05$.

Among 10,000 people, expected counts are 100 diseased and 9,900 without disease. Expect 99 true positives, but $9900\times0.05=495$ false positives. Among 594 positive results:

$$P(D\mid+)=\frac{99}{99+495}=\frac16\approx16.67\%.$$

There is no paradox: the disease-free group is large. Even a small false-positive rate produces many false positives. These are expected counts; a real sample would fluctuate around them.

<Lab kind="bayes" lang="en" />

Hold sensitivity and specificity fixed, then increase prevalence. Positive predictive value changes. Conditional test properties therefore do not determine this probability for every population. In practice, sensitivity and specificity can themselves vary across contexts.

## A transparent implementation

```python
p, sensitivity, specificity = 0.01, 0.99, 0.95
true_positive = p * sensitivity
false_positive = (1-p) * (1-specificity)
posterior = true_positive / (true_positive + false_positive)
print(round(posterior, 6))  # 0.166667
```

Each term is a proportion of the whole population. Their sum in the denominator is $P(+)$. Check that it is strictly positive before conditioning.

## Exercises

1. Recalculate for 10% prevalence with the same test.
2. On a fair die, let $A$ mean “even” and $B$ mean “greater than 4”. Are they independent?
3. Why is the false-positive rate different from $P(D^c\mid+)$?
4. After two positive tests, can you just square the first posterior probability?

<summary>Worked solutions</summary>



1. True positives represent $0.1\times0.99=0.099$, and false positives $0.9\times0.05=0.045$. The answer is $0.099/0.144=0.6875$, or 68.75%.
2. $P(A)=1/2$, $P(B)=1/3$, and $P(A\cap B)=P(\{6\})=1/6$. The product also equals $1/6$, so the events are independent.
3. $P(+\mid D^c)$ uses people without disease as its reference group. $P(D^c\mid+)$ uses positive people. Their denominators differ.
4. No. You need the joint distribution of results, including any conditional independence assumption. Even under conditional independence, multiply conditional likelihoods and renormalize using Bayes rather than squaring the posterior.
