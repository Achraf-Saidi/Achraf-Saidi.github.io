## A data point can be a vector

Describing an observation by height and mass requires two ordered numbers. A vector $x=(x_1,x_2)$ is an element of $\mathbb R^2$, with coordinates $x_1,x_2$. Geometrically, draw it as a displacement from the origin. In data science, interpret it as a list of measurements. This dual view connects calculation and representation.

Prerequisite: [algebra](/achraf-notebooks/en/foundations/algebra/). Goals: compute linear combinations and dot products, read matrix dimensions, explain matrix–vector multiplication and compose transformations.

## Adding and measuring

Addition acts coordinate by coordinate: $(1,2)+(3,-1)=(4,1)$. Multiplication by a real scalar $\alpha$ gives $\alpha(1,2)=(\alpha,2\alpha)$. A **linear combination** of vectors $u,v$ has the form $au+bv$ with real coefficients $a,b$. The vectors $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$ express any $x=(x_1,x_2)$ as $x_1e_1+x_2e_2$.

The **dot product** $u^Tv=\sum_{j=1}^d u_jv_j$ produces a number from two vectors with $d$ coordinates; $\sum$ means summing terms indexed by $j$ from 1 to $d$. The superscript $T$ denotes transpose, turning a column into a row. For $u=(1,2)$ and $v=(3,-1)$, $u^Tv=3-2=1$.

The Euclidean norm is $\|u\|=\sqrt{u^Tu}$. For $(3,4)$ it equals 5, as in Pythagoras’ theorem. Nonzero vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero. With measurements in different units, this numerical geometry depends on scaling: converting centimetres to metres changes distances unless scaling is handled.

## A matrix specifies what happens to the axes

A matrix $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$ has $m$ rows and $d$ columns. It maps a vector with $d$ coordinates to one with $m$ coordinates. Take

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad x=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}.$$

Dot each row of $A$ with $x$:

$$Ax=\begin{pmatrix}1\times3+2\times4\\0\times3+1\times4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}11\\4\end{pmatrix}.$$

An equally useful interpretation: $Ax$ is a combination of the **columns** of $A$, weighted by coordinates of $x$: $3(1,0)+4(2,1)=(11,4)$. These columns are $Ae_1$ and $Ae_2$. Knowing the images of the basis vectors therefore determines the entire linear transformation.

The figure shows the transformed unit square and basis vectors. If the determinant $ad-bc$ of a $2\times2$ matrix is zero, the square collapses onto a line or point. The transformation loses a dimension and cannot be inverted. The determinant’s sign indicates orientation; its absolute value gives the area scale factor.

## Multiplication means composition

If $A$ maps dimension $d$ to $m$, and $B$ maps dimension $m$ to $k$, then $BA$ has size $k\times d$ and $BAx=B(Ax)$. The inner dimensions must agree. Its row-$i$, column-$j$ entry is $(BA)_{ij}=\sum_{r=1}^m B_{ir}A_{rj}$.

Order matters. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $AB=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ whereas $BA=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. A shear followed by a stretch need not match the reverse order.

## Computing transparently

```python
A = [[1, 2], [0, 1]]
x = [3, 4]
y = [sum(a*v for a, v in zip(row, x)) for row in A]
print(y)  # [11, 4]
```

With NumPy, `np.array(A) @ np.array(x)` performs this product. The `*` operator means elementwise multiplication, not matrix multiplication. In R, matrix multiplication uses `%*%`; `*` is also elementwise. Always inspect array shapes.
