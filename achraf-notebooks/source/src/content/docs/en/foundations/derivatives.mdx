## Measuring speed at one instant

A position is given by $s(t)=t^2$, with time $t$ in seconds and position $s$ in metres. Between $t=1$ and $t=2$, average speed is $(4-1)/(2-1)=3\ \mathrm{m/s}$. But this average does not reveal the speed exactly at $t=1$. We approach that question by comparing increasingly close times.

Prerequisite: [limits and continuity](/achraf-notebooks/en/foundations/limits/). Goals: construct a derivative from a limit, interpret a tangent, recognize nondifferentiability and apply the chain rule.

## The difference quotient

For a real-valued function $f$, a point $x$, and a displacement $h\ne0$, the quotient

$$q_h(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

measures output change per unit input change. The second input is $x+h$; the vertical change is $f(x+h)-f(x)$. Geometrically, this quotient is the slope of the **secant** through the two graph points.

For $f(x)=x^2$, expand before taking any limit:

$$q_h(x)=\frac{x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{h(2x+h)}{h}=2x+h.$$

Cancellation uses $h\ne0$. We have never divided by zero. At $x=1$, the displacements $h=1,0.1,0.01$ give slopes 3, 2.1 and 2.01. Negative displacements also give slopes approaching 2.

## The definition and the tangent

If the quotient approaches the same finite real limit from both sides, $f$ is **differentiable at $x$**, with derivative

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$

The prime $'$ denotes a derivative; another notation is $\mathrm df/\mathrm dx$. For squaring, $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h)=2x$. The derivative is itself a function assigning each point its local slope.

The tangent at input $a$ has equation $y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$. Here $a$ is fixed, $x$ ranges along the line and $y$ is its height. For the square at $a=1$, this becomes $y=1+2(x-1)=2x-1$.

## A local approximation with a remainder

For small $h$, write $f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h$. For squaring at $a=1$, with $h=0.02$, this predicts $1+2(0.02)=1.04$, whereas the exact value is $1.0404$. The discrepancy $0.0004=h^2$ shrinks faster than $|h|$.

Precisely, $f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+r(h)$, where the remainder satisfies $r(h)/h\to0$ as $h\to0$. This is what makes differentiability more specific than a line merely looking close to the graph.

## Why continuity is insufficient

For $f(x)=|x|$ at zero, the quotient is $|h|/h$. It equals 1 on the right and $-1$ on the left. The function is continuous but not differentiable at zero: no common finite slope exists. A graph without a hole need not have a derivative everywhere.

## Rules built from the definition

A constant $c$ has derivative zero since $(c-c)/h=0$. For $f(x)=ax+b$, the quotient equals $a$, so $f'(x)=a$. Limit laws establish linearity: for differentiable functions $u,v$, $(u+v)'=u'+v'$ and $(cu)'=cu'$. The product rule is $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$, not $u'v'$.

For a composition $y=g(f(x))$, the **chain rule** gives $(g\circ f)'(x)=g'(f(x))f'(x)$, provided both functions are differentiable at the relevant points. Intuitively, a small change in $x$ changes $f$ by approximately $f'(x)$ times that change; $g$ then multiplies that intermediate change by $g'(f(x))$. The factors multiply.

For $(x^2+1)^2$, the inside $u=x^2+1$ has derivative $2x$; the outside $u^2$ has derivative $2u$. The answer is $2(x^2+1)\times2x=4x(x^2+1)$. This mechanism becomes backpropagation.

## Computing with Python

```python
def f(x):
    return x*x

def derivative_forward(x, h):
    if h == 0:
        raise ValueError("h must be nonzero")
    return (f(x+h)-f(x))/h

for h in [1.0, 0.1, 0.01]:
    print(h, derivative_forward(1.0, h))
```

Automatic differentiation libraries such as PyTorch apply differentiation rules to a graph of operations; they do not usually estimate this quotient at a small $h$. Finite differences remain useful for independent gradient checks.
