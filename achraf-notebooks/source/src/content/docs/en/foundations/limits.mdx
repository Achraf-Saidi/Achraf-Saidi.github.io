## Calculating around a hole

Consider $f(x)=(x^2-1)/(x-1)$. At $x=1$ this quotient is undefined because its denominator is zero. But for $x\ne1$, factoring gives $f(x)=x+1$. At $x=0.9$ the output is 1.9; at $x=0.99$, 1.99; at $x=1.01$, 2.01. Outputs approach 2 as inputs approach 1. This says something different from evaluating the function at that point.

Prerequisites: [functions](/achraf-notebooks/en/foundations/functions/) and factoring. Goals: interpret limits, distinguish limits from evaluation, identify discontinuities and read a precise definition.

## Reading the notation

The expression $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ means “the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ is $L$”. Here $a$ is the input being approached and $L$ the output being approached. We consider $x$ **different from** $a$, sufficiently close to $a$, and in the domain of $f$.

In our example, $\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=2$ although $f(1)$ is undefined. A limit does not require the value at the point to exist. Numerical tables suggest a limit but do not prove it: a function could behave differently between sampled inputs.

## “As close as desired”, precisely

For a function defined near $a$, except possibly at $a$, the definition is:

$$\forall\varepsilon>0,\quad\exists\delta>0,\quad 0<|x-a|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.$$

The symbol $\forall$ means “for every”; $\exists$ means “there exists”. The positive number $\varepsilon$ specifies an output tolerance. We choose a positive input distance $\delta$. Absolute-value bars measure distance; the implication $\Longrightarrow$ means that the left-hand condition guarantees the right-hand condition. We must handle **every** positive output tolerance, however small.

For $f(x)=x+1$, $a=1$ and $L=2$, we have $|f(x)-2|=|x-1|$. Choosing $\delta=\varepsilon$ therefore suffices. If the input is within $\delta$ of 1, the output is within $\varepsilon$ of 2. This is a proof because it works for an arbitrary tolerance rather than a few selected examples.

## Both sides must agree

For $g(x)=|x|/x$ with $x\ne0$, outputs equal $-1$ to the left of zero and 1 to its right. The left-hand limit is $-1$; the right-hand limit is 1. A finite two-sided limit cannot exist when these disagree. The values do not “balance out” to give a limit of zero.

Saying a function tends to $+\infty$ means that it eventually exceeds every positive threshold as inputs approach in the specified direction. Infinity here is not a real number to manipulate like 2 or 3.

## Continuity joins the limit to the value

A function is **continuous at $a$** if it is defined there and $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$. Our original function has a hole at 1. Define a new function $F$ by $F(x)=f(x)$ for $x\ne1$ and $F(1)=2$. This extension is continuous at 1. Setting $F(1)=5$ would preserve the limit but prevent continuity.

Continuity is weaker than differentiability: $|x|$ is continuous at zero but has a corner there. We will verify this by calculating its slopes.

## A numerical experiment

```python
for h in [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    x = 1 + h
    direct = (x*x - 1) / (x - 1)
    stable = x + 1
    print(h, direct, stable)
```

The two calculations agree mathematically. Numerically, subtracting close values can lose precision; shrinking $h$ indefinitely does not necessarily improve a computer calculation.

## Exercises

1. Calculate $\lim_{x\to3}(2x+1)$ and justify it using tolerances.
2. Let $f(x)=2$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=7$. Find the limit at zero. Is the function continuous there?
3. Why does direct substitution into $(x^2-1)/(x-1)$ not settle the limit?

<details>
<summary>Worked solutions</summary>



1. The limit is 7. Since $|(2x+1)-7|=2|x-3|$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon/2$.
2. The limit is 2: every neighbouring output equals 2. The function is not continuous because $f(0)=7\ne2$.
3. Substitution gives $0/0$, which is not a value. Study neighbouring inputs instead: simplification for $x\ne1$ reveals the limit 2.
