Survival, censoring and calibration: the research problem

Event and censoring times, IPCW, different kinds of drift and the difficult assumptions behind anytime-valid monitoring.

Method

Define the variables. Re-derive each equation. Reproduce the example independently. Test the code. Explain assumptions and limitations.

01 — The target of a survival model

A patient has features X and a latent event time T≥0, such as time to recurrence. Conditional survival is S(t|X)=P(T>t|X). The cumulative event risk by horizon τ is F(τ|X)=1−S(τ|X) for a single event type. When defined, hazard is λ(t|X)=lim₍Δ→0⁺₎P(t≤T<t+Δ|T≥t,X)/Δ. Hazard is a rate, not a probability; its numerical value need not be at most one.

Discrimination measures whether the model ranks higher-risk patients above lower-risk ones. Calibration asks whether a predicted risk, say 20% over three years, corresponds to about 20% events among comparable cases. Excellent ranking can coexist with systematic risk overestimation, which can alter clinical decisions.

02 — Censoring hides the label

Often we observe only U=min(T,C) and Δ=1{T≤C}, where C is censoring time and ties count as observed events. If Δ=1, the event happened at U. If Δ=0, all we know is T>U. This is not the same as lifetime absence of an event.

For prediction horizon τ, the binary outcome Yτ=1{T≤τ} is known if an event is observed by τ, or follow-up extends past τ without an event. It is unknown if censoring occurs before τ without a prior event. Treating that missing outcome as zero distorts event frequency.

Define censoring survival G(t|X)=P(C≥t|X), using a clear convention for ties. Kaplan–Meier may estimate marginal censoring survival under strong enough independence assumptions; informative censoring can require conditional modeling. IPCW requires an appropriate conditional independence assumption and positivity G(t|X)>0 on the horizon.

03 — Building a fixed-horizon score

Let pᵢ=F̂(τ|Xᵢ) be predicted event probability. With complete outcomes, a basic marginal calibration residual is n⁻¹Σᵢ(Yᵢτ−pᵢ). Censoring hides some labels. Under suitable assumptions, inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting can estimate event frequency by F̂IPCW(τ)=n⁻¹ΣᵢΔᵢ1{Uᵢ≤τ}/Ĝ(Uᵢ−|Xᵢ), using the left-limit convention for observed event times. Compare this to n⁻¹Σᵢpᵢ to assess mean calibration.

For a fixed-horizon Brier loss, observed events before τ are weighted by inverse censoring survival at event time; individuals known event-free after τ receive inverse survival at τ; early-censored individuals are not assigned fabricated outcomes. Competing risks, dependent censoring, extreme weights and estimating G on the same patients complicate the validity of estimators.

04 — Why drift and censoring must be separated

Consider a model initially validated and then deployed. Three different changes may occur: (1) covariate distribution Pₜ(X) shifts without necessarily altering conditional risk; (2) outcome mechanism Pₜ(T|X) changes and may cause miscalibration; (3) censoring and follow-up Pₜ(C|X,T) changes, altering observed residuals without any change in true survival risk. These mechanisms may coexist.

An alarm based on uncorrected labels may react to follow-up policy rather than deteriorating clinical prediction. Conversely, misspecified censoring corrections can hide genuine drift. A sound research design simulates the mechanisms separately, measures false alarms while the model remains calibrated, and measures detection delay under true miscalibration.

05 — E-processes: guarantees and open questions

Suppose a sequence of scores ξₜ∈[−1,1] satisfies E[ξₜ|ℱₜ₋₁]≤0 under the null. For a prechosen betting coefficient λ≥0, Hoeffding's lemma ensures eₜ=exp(λξₜ−λ²/2) has conditional expectation at most one. Therefore Eₙ=∏ₜ₌₁ⁿeₜ is a nonnegative supermartingale and Ville's inequality yields P₀(supₙEₙ≥1/α)≤α. This is the foundation of optional-stopping-valid monitoring.

Important: raw IPCW residuals are generally not bounded by one because 1/G can be large. Moreover, estimating G and receiving outcomes with delay can break the required conditional expectation property. Clipping, independent nuisance estimation or confidence bounds requires further proof and may change the target. An IPCW residual does not become a valid e-process automatically: establishing such validity is part of the research problem.

06 — Reproducible code

This program runs with Python and NumPy on an ordinary computer. It is an educational laboratory, not a clinical or production system.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, horizon = 12000, 2.0
# Toy exponential event/censoring times: independent by construction.
event = rng.exponential(scale=3.0, size=n)
censor = rng.exponential(scale=4.0, size=n)
observed = np.minimum(event, censor)
delta = (event <= censor)
# Known censoring survival G(t)=exp(-t/4) ONLY in this simulation.
G_event = np.exp(-observed/4.0)
observed_events = delta & (observed <= horizon)
ipcw_event_rate = np.mean(observed_events / G_event)
true_event_rate = np.mean(event <= horizon)
theoretical_rate = 1 - np.exp(-horizon/3.0)
print("Naive observed events:", observed_events.mean())
print("IPCW:", ipcw_event_rate)
print("Complete-data simulation truth:", true_event_rate)
print("Population risk:", theoretical_rate)
# This is a FIXED-SAMPLE estimator, not an anytime-valid e-process.

07 — Solved exercise

A patient is censored at month 8; the prediction horizon is month 12. May Y12 be set to zero? What about a patient known event-free at month 13? Why can division by G(t) be unstable?

Reveal the reasoning and solution

No: censoring at month 8 hides whether the event occurred by month 12. Yes, event-free follow-up through month 13 establishes Y12=0. Tiny G produces extreme weights, high variance and potential violations of the bounds needed for e-processes.
