Research

Reliable prediction under change.

My doctoral direction sits at the intersection of calibration, sequential inference, survival analysis and machine learning. The central question is how to monitor and maintain trustworthy predictive performance when data arrive over time and outcomes may be delayed or censored.

Core research problem

Many predictive models are evaluated once on a fixed test set. Real deployments are different: populations change, clinical practice evolves, covariate distributions shift and outcomes can arrive late. A model that was well calibrated at deployment may therefore become unreliable.

The research challenge becomes harder in time-to-event settings. With censoring, the true outcome at a fixed horizon may not yet be observed for every individual. Standard corrections such as inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting depend on a censoring model, and misspecification can itself distort the apparent calibration signal.

Research directions

Sequential calibration

Methods that allow repeated monitoring of predictive calibration over time while controlling false alarms.

Censoring-aware monitoring

Valid monitoring procedures for survival outcomes, including uncertainty introduced by estimating the censoring mechanism.

Adaptive nuisance estimation

Studying how flexible statistical or machine-learning models for nuisance quantities can be integrated without invalidating sequential guarantees.

Medical prediction

Applications where reliable risk estimates matter operationally and where population shift, delayed outcomes and heterogeneous subgroups are realistic.

Methods I work with

Sequential inference and e-processes, calibration diagnostics, survival analysis, IPCW, statistical learning, machine learning, deep learning, simulation studies, optimization and reproducible computational experiments.
